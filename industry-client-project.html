Industry Client Project

Management Skills · Rutgers Business School

A consulting-style engagement with a 10-person car detailing business: diagnosing why employees were repeatedly arriving late and recommending how management could fix it.

TimelineSep 2025 – Dec 2025
Team5 students
ClientLocal detailing business, NJ
MethodsInterviews, review analysis, frameworks

The Problem

The client is a founder-run detailing shop with ten employees and a strong reputation, backed by more than a hundred positive online reviews. Management told us that one team member had been late to work at least four times, and nothing had happened as a result. Because each detailing job is time-intensive, a late start meant rescheduled appointments, frustrated customers, coworkers covering extra work, and lost revenue for the day.

Approach

  • Primary research: four interviews with management by phone, email, in person, and over Zoom, covering conduct, communication, staffing, and leadership style.
  • Secondary research: analysis of Google and Yelp reviews and of the social media presence of the client and its competitors.
  • Frameworks: Expectancy Theory and the Team Effectiveness Model, used to explain why the problem was happening rather than only describing it.

Findings

Our analysis traced the tardiness to a missing system rather than to one person. Rewards were handed out informally and without clear criteria, and lateness carried no consequences. Under Expectancy Theory, employees had no clear link between effort, performance, and outcome. Under the Team Effectiveness Model, the shop had no SMART goals for attendance or workflow, which opened the door to social loafing and pushed extra work onto teammates.

The deeper issue was a "family-style" culture that had served the business well at a small size but needed more formal, performance-based structure as it grew.

Recommendations

1. Improve the quality of management decisions

Adopt a structured decision process (the Bazerman problem-definition steps) and run pre-mortems (the Klein/Osland method) so managers define problems clearly, weigh criteria, and anticipate failure before acting. Benchmarked against Amazon's written-planning culture and the U.S. Army's structured planning and after-action reviews.

Trade-offs we flagged: training cost, inconsistent use, and the risk of slowing decisions down.

2. Build a formal influence and communication strategy

Train managers in interpersonal influence (reciprocity, social proof, consistency, authority) and procedural influence (clear agendas, group norms, decision rules). Benchmarked against Google's "influence without authority" training and Netflix's transparent decision rules.

Trade-offs we flagged: manager discomfort, training cost, and the risk of eroding trust if autonomy goes too far.

Together, the first recommendation improves the quality of decisions and the second improves how well they are communicated and carried out. We also proposed using authority (research and real-world evidence) and social proof (what high-performing companies do) to help the owner accept the changes.

What I Took Away

  • How to turn interviews and public data into a clear problem statement.
  • How to use frameworks to find root causes and process gaps instead of blaming individuals.
  • How to weigh costs and risks of each recommendation before proposing it.
